The model simultaneously considers the following two conflicting optimization objectives:
\[\begin{cases}
\min T(x)=\frac{M}{P_{\mathrm{SES}}+xP_{\mathrm{launch}}} \\
\min C(x)=C_{\mathrm{total}}(x) & 
\end{cases}\]

\subsection{Improvement of scheme c}
\subsubsection{Rocket Iteration: Transportation cost iteration}
\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Theoretical Basis}\par
When analyzing the evolution of transportation costs per 100kg payload, the core concept in industrial economics—the Learning Curve Model—must be introduced.
That is Wright's Law.The production cost of a unit product It does not fluctuate randomly, but shows a predictable power law downward trend as cumulative production increases.In the field of commercial aerospace, this effect is further modified into a cost iteration mechanism driven by both "economies of scale" and "reuse frequency".
Therefore, this study introduces the dual-factor cost structure, the unit mass cost of a single launch is deconstructed into two components with very different properties: the rocket body manufacturing and operating cost $C_{body}$, which is affected by the industrial learning curve, and the relatively rigid fuel (propellant) cost $C_{fuel}$, which is affected by chemical industry pricing.
According to Wright's Law in industrial economics, rocket body manufacturing and launch operation costs have significant "learning by doing" characteristics, that is, as the cumulative launch volume and the number of reuses increase, this cost decreases with a power law. 
Therefore, the cost dynamics equation should be expressed as:
\[C_{launch}=C_{man}(n)+C_{fuel}=C_1\cdot n^{-b}+C_{fixed}\]
\indent Among them, $n$ is the cumulative output, $b$ is the learning index, and $C_{fixed}$ represents the thermodynamic cost floor determined by chemical energy density.
Model calculations show that while manufacturing costs can be rapidly amortized through a high learning rate, fuel costs constitute an insurmountable horizontal asymptote.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Parameter Settings}\par
Based on the analysis of existing technical paths, this model sets the following core parameters: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item Manufacturing cost decay rate: Taking into account the scale effect and the maturity of reuse technology, the average annual decay coefficient $\alpha$ of the rocket body manufacturing and operation part is set to 5\%.
  \item Fuel cost rigidity: The fuel cost shared per 100kg payload is set to a constant $C_{fuel}$ as the convergence limit of cost evolution.load is set to a constant $C_{fuel}$ as the convergence limit of cost evolution.load is set to a constant $C_{fuel}$ as the convergence limit of cost evolution.
\end{itemize}

\indent Taken together, future unit transportation costs will no longer approach zero infinitely, but will approach the bottom line of fuel costs along an exponential curve. This processing method suppresses the distortion risk of pure mathematical models in long-term predictions and is consistent with the physical reality of large-scale logistics networks.\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Model Construction}\par
Based on the above two-factor analysis, this study establishes the final iterative formula for the evolution of cargo transportation cost $C_{launch}(t)$ per 100kg over time $t$:
Let $t$ be the year (taking the model starting year as $t = 0$), $C_{launch}(t)$ consists of the variable manufacturing part $C_{man}(t)$ and the fixed fuel part $C_{fuel}$, then there is:
\[C_{launch}(t)=C_{man_0}\times(1-\alpha)^t+C_{fuel}\]
Bringing in the parameters:ng in the parameters:ng in the parameters:
\[C_{launch}(t)=(C_{total_0}-C_{fuel})\times(0.95)^t+C_{fuel}\]
\indent This formula clearly describes the two stages of cost decline: in the near term (when $t$ is small), it is dominated by $(1 - \alpha)^t$, which is reflected in the rapid decline of costs, consistent with the exponential characteristics of Moore's Law; in the long term (when $t$ tends to infinity), $C_{launch}(t)$ asymptotically converges to $C_{fuel}$, reflecting the ultimate constraint of energy prices on the economics of space logistics.

\subsubsection{A new strategy based on the iterative models}
\vspace{0.3em}
Based on the above iterative model, we propose a new hybrid transportation strategy to further optimize the trade-off between transportation costs and time. Building upon the HCTO-PM model, we introduce a dynamic cost adjustment mechanism by incorporating the time variable $t$ to reflect the evolution of rocket launch costs over time, resulting in
Multi-Year Hybrid Capacity Trade-off Optimization with Pareto Mapping (MY-HCTO-PM) model. \\
Calculation formulas as follow:
\[P_{SES}T+P_{launch}\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{T}x_{(t)} = M\]
\[C_{total}=C_{SES}\cdot P_{SES}\cdot T+\sum_{t=0}^{T}C_{launch}(t)\cdot x_{(t)}\cdot P_{launch}\]
\indent For each feasible $T$, we first calculate the total mass transported by rockets and then allocate it in reverse chronological order (starting from the last year) to take advantage of manufacturing cost decay benefits. This generates a set of $(T,C)$ data points, i.e., the Pareto frontier.
To facilitate decision-making, we define three representative solutions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Balanced solution: Use the normalized time and cost to calculate the Euclidean distance from each feasible point to the ideal point $(T_{min}, C_{min})$. The one with the smallest distance is the balanced solution.
  \item Radical solution (Faster): The equilibrium solution shifts to the left by a certain proportion (reduces the total construction period and allows the cost to increase)
  \item Economic solution (Cheaper): The equilibrium solution shifts to the right by a certain proportion (extends the total construction period to reduce costs)
\end{itemize}
\indent This method ensures that all three types of solutions fall within the optimal compromise range, and can intuitively demonstrate the trade-off relationship between construction period and cost.

\subsection{Introduction of Non-ideal Factors}

The core of non-ideal factors is "system failure leading to capacity loss, time extension, and cost increase." It is necessary to combine aerospace engineering practice and equipment reliability rules to transform abstract faults into quantifiable parameters to ensure the operability and scientificity of the model.

\subsubsection{Non-ideal Factors in Space Elevator System}

The non-ideal factors of the space elevator system mainly stem from the dynamic characteristics and mechanical system losses of the ultra-long cable (100,000 kilometers of graphene cable). 
The specific quantification is as follows:inly stem from the dynamic characteristics and mechanical system losses of the ultra-long cable (100,000 kilometers of graphene cable). The specific quantification is as follows:

\textbf{Cable shaking:}Affected by the disturbance of the earth's orbit and the resistance of the upper atmosphere, the cable will produce low-frequency shaking (amplitude is about 5-10 kilometers). \textbf{Repair cost:r cost:}Fault repair involves cable inspection, mechanical component replacement, etc. The annual maintenance cost accounts for 5\% of the annual transportation cost under perfect working conditions.


\subsubsection{Non-ideal Factors in Rocket Transportation Systems}

Traditional rocket transportation non-ideal factors mainly stem from the complexity of the launch process and environmental dependencies, with specific quantification as follows:

\textbf{Launch failure:}The structural complexity of heavy rockets (such as advanced Falcon Heavy) is high. Referring to historical data on space launches, the launch success rate is defined as \(p_{launch} = 0.95\);

\textbf{Launch site efficiency decline:}Launch sites are affected by equipment maintenance and weather conditions (such as typhoons, thunderstorms). The launch site availability rate is defined as \(r_{launch} = 0.85\), which leads to a reduction in the annual maximum number of launches at the launch site~\cite{nasa_weather_07R}.

\textbf{Additional costs:ional costs:}After a rocket launch fails, the lost material requires the rocket to be relaunched to meet the load capacity requirement. The increase in total rocket carrying capacity and rocket replenishment bring new costs, ignoring the rocket construction cost caused by rocket damage.

\noindent\textbf{Space elevator system optimization:}
\begin{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Rocket transportation system optimization:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Improve rocket recovery and reuse technology to increase launch success rate\(p_{launch}\), thereby reducing reshipment costs; at the same time, optimize the launch site scheduling system to increase launch site availability\(r_{launch}\) and reduce rocket launch costs;
  \item Control the annual launch frequency of rockets within 8,500 times to avoid overdrafting the life of the launch site and balance short-term capacity and long-term sustainability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Preparation of models}
This chapter aims to construct a multi-dimensional dynamic mass balance model (DMS model) to systematically evaluate the total water demand of a lunar colony with a population of 100,000 in full operation for one year.
Due to the extreme vacuum, microgravity (1/6g) and huge temperature difference between day and night in the lunar environment, water resource management will present system dynamics that are completely different from those on the Earth~\cite{water_electrolysis_2013}.

The model will take into account human metabolism, intensive controlled ecological agriculture, industrial utilities and inevitable technological leaks.
By extrapolating the existing Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) specifications of the International Space Station (ISS) and combining them with data from closed ecosystem experiments such as Biosphere 2 and Lunar Palace 1, the report will quantify the annual water deficit produced by a lunar colony within the constraints of closed-loop recycling efficiency, thereby determining the minimum mass of supplies that must be transported from Earth to the Moon each year.
According to the previous simulation calculation based on Pareto optimal equilibrium solution,

\subsection{Water Demand Model}
\subsubsection{Physiological and Domestic Water Demand}

\noindent\textbf{Water Flux Analysis for Maintaining Survival and Quality of Life}\par
Based on the experience of the International Space Station, the daily basic physiological water consumption of each astronaut (including drinking, food rehydration and basic hygiene) is about 3.8 liters (1 gallon)~\cite{nasa_iss_water_recovery}.
However, for a long-term operating colony, in order to maintain the mental health and social functions of residents, domestic water standards must be closer to the normal state of the earth.
The average daily indoor water consumption per person in the average American household is about 227 to 375 liters, covering showering, laundry and toilet flushing.
In the lunar environment, the circulating flux of domestic water $W_{dom}$ can be expressed by the following formula:
\[W_{dom}=P\times(q_{drink}+q_{hygiene}+q_{laundry}+q_{food})\]
\indent Among them, $P = 100000$, $q_i$ represents the per capita daily demand coefficient of each sub-item.
This study sets the "lunar colonization standard" per capita daily demand at 35 liters, which is much higher than the maintenance level of the ISS, but only one-tenth of the wasteful consumption on earth.

Therefore, based on the current state-of-the-art recovery rate, we can estimate that by 2050, the recovery rate of the water recovery system usable in lunar colonies will reach 99\%.
The daily deficit of domestic water $L_{dom}$ satisfies:
\[L_{dom}=W_{dom}\times(1-\eta_{WRS})\]
\indent Based on the recovery rate of 99\% expected to be achieved after the lunar base is fully completed, a population of 100,000 will lose approximately 35,000 liters of water per day in the life cycle. These losses are mainly due to the saturated replacement of the filter bed, irreversible binding during the catalytic oxidation process, and unextractable bound water in the solid residue of excreta~\cite{nelson2009water}.

\subsubsection{Agricultural Water Demand}
\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Water Footprint of Controlled Environmental Agriculture (CEA)}\par
A colony with a population of 100,000 cannot rely on dehydrated food transported from the earth for a long time, and a large-scale lunar greenhouse must be established.
The Bioregenerative Life Support System (BLSS) provides food and oxygen through photosynthesis of plants and participates in the water cycle as a natural condensed water collector.
According to Biosphere 2 and NASA research, the agricultural area required to provide 100\% food self-sufficiency is approximately 370 square meters per person~\cite{vpk_moon_water_2024}. For a population of 100,000, the total area amounts to 37 million square meters.
In a hydroponic or aeroponic system, although it saves 90\% of water than soil agriculture, due to the high transpiration rate of the plants, the amount of water circulated within the system is staggering.
The agricultural irrigation intensity $q_{irr}$ is usually about 3.8 liters per square meter per day. Then the total agricultural cycle flux $W_{agri_flux}$ is:
\[W_{agri_flux}=A_{total}\times q_{irr}\approx1.4\times10^8\mathrm{L/day}\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Balance Between Transpiration and Humidity Recovery}\par
In controlled environment agriculture, most of the water absorbed by plants is released into the air through leaf transpiration.
In order to close the water cycle, this humidity condensate must be recovered through a condensation heat exchanger.
The net loss rate $(1 - \eta_{agri})$ of the agricultural cycle is usually much lower than that of the domestic water cycle because most of the water flows in the pure physical transpiration state.
However, losses arise from three main areas:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Biomass fixed water, that is, the water contained in the plant tissue itself, is transferred to human metabolic circuits during harvest and consumption;
\item Regular emptying and cleaning of root solutions (due to mineral accumulation);
\item Leaks during greenhouse maintenance.
\end{enumerate}
The recovery rate of the high-efficiency agricultural system is set to 99.7\%, and the average daily agricultural loss is:
\[L_{agri}=W_{agri_flux}\times0.003\approx420,000\mathrm{L/day}\]

\subsubsection{Technical Leaks and Environmental Damage}
\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Airlock Recirculation and Extravehicular Activity (EVA)}\par
In a city of 100,000 inhabitants, frequent opening and closing of airlocks is the norm. About 4\% of the air remains in each cycle and escapes to vacuum.
Since the air is maintained at a relative humidity of about 50\%, these escaping gases carry a non-negligible amount of water vapor.
The airlock daily deficit $L_{leak_air}$ can be modeled as:
\[L_{leak_air}=N_{EVA}\times V_{lock}\times\rho_{air}\times\omega\times(1-\eta_{reclaim})\]
\indent Where $N_{EVA}$ is the number of daily cycles, $V_{lock}$ is the airlock volume, and $\omega$ is the absolute humidity.\\

\noindent\textbf{Structural Background Leakage}\par
The vast surface area of ​​lunar habitats results in tiny structural penetrations.
Even if it is built to the highest aerospace standards, the annual gas loss rate per cubic meter of volume may still reach 0.01\%~\cite{abney_sabatier_h2}.
For a colony with a volume of 3 million cubic meters, the annual water content loss due to this background leakage is constant.

\textbf{Dual system comprehensive scheme} Based on the MY-HCTO-PM model logic, this scheme adopts the principle of "marginal cost minimization". Model results show that agricultural and industrial losses account for more than 94\% of total recharge needs. \section{Modeling and Solving of Problem 4}
\subsection{Preparation of models}
Traditional cost-time optimization models often ignore the cumulative effect of high-frequency heavy rocket launches on the stratosphere and global climate.
In order to evaluate the impact of the monthly material transportation demand of up to $10^8$ tons anywhere on the earth's atmospheric environment, this chapter introduces environmental impact factors based on life cycle assessment (LCA) on the basis of the original logistics optimization model, and establishes a threshold constraint mechanism based on the atmospheric carrying capacity, aiming to quantitatively analyze the ecological costs of different transportation strategies.

\subsection{Construction of LCA Model}
In traditional surface transportation LCA analysis, environmental impact is usually mainly characterized by carbon dioxide $CO_2$ emissions.
However, space transportation has its own uniqueness: rocket engines directly inject combustion products into the stratosphere and mesosphere.
Due to the lack of convective weather phenomena (such as rainfall) at these altitudes, the residence time of particulate matter can last for years.
Therefore, the single launch environmental impact equivalent ($\$mu $) defined in this model is a composite indicator, and its calculation formula is as follows:
\[\mu=\alpha\cdot M_{CO_2}+\beta\cdot M_{BC}+\gamma\cdot M_{\mathrm{Al_2O_3}}+\delta\cdot M_{H_2O}\]
\indent Where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $M_{CO_2}$ is the greenhouse gas emission mass.
\item $M_{BC}$ is the mass of black carbon, soot particles.
\item $M_{\mathrm{Al_2O_3}}$ is the mass of aluminum oxide particles (mainly from solid boosters).
\item $M_{H_2O}$ is the water vapor in the upper atmosphere.
\item $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ are the environmental destruction weight coefficients for each pollutant.
\end{itemize}

In the numerical simulation, we simplified the above complex physical and chemical processes into a comprehensive scalar $ENV_{IMPACT\_PER\_LAUNCH}$ (set to 2500 EIU), which represents the comprehensive marginal damage to the global environment caused by the launch of a heavy-lift launch vehicle (such as Starship class).
\subsection{Calculation of Total Environmental Impact}
\subsubsection{Physical Derivation of Atmospheric Carrying Capacity Threshold}
The total environmental impact $E_{total}$ of the system is a function of time $T$.Since the space elevator relies mainly on electricity during its operation phase (assumed to be powered by clean energy), its direct environmental emissions are considered zero (or negligible relative to rockets).
Therefore, the total environmental impact is dominated by rocket launch activities.
Assuming $L_t$ is the number of rocket launches in year $t$, the total environmental load of the system is calculated as follows:
\[E_{total}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^T(L_t\times\mu)\]
\indent Where $L_t$ is endogenously derived from the economic dispatch strategy determined by the current year’s remaining transportation demand $M_{remain}(t)$ and rocket manufacturing/fuel costs.
This formula reveals the strong coupling relationship between environmental impact and transportation strategy: the pursuit of shortening the total construction period $T$ will inevitably lead to a surge in $L_t$ in the early stage, resulting in an exponential increase in $E_{total}$.
This paper derives the maximum safe annual emissivity based on the steady-state load of stratospheric aerosols.
The natural background aerosols in the Earth's stratosphere are mainly composed of sulfates, and their total mass is relatively stable.

We define the following core parameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item $M_{bg}$:Total stratospheric background aerosol mass,($6 \times 10^8$kg).
\item $\eta $: Maximum allowable perturbation ratio (Perturbation Ratio).
\item $\tau $: Average residence time of particles (Residence Time).
\item $m_{eff}$: Effective particle injection volume in a single shot($2 \times 10^3$kg).
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Steady-state Equilibrium Calculation Formula}
\indent When launch activities continue for a long time, the mass $M_{human}$ of man-made pollutants in the stratosphere will reach a dynamic balance, that is, the annual injection amount is equal to the deposition amount.\\
The equilibrium equation is:
\[M_{human}=C_{atm}\times m_{eff}\times\tau\]
According to environmental safety constraints:
\[M_{human}\leq M_{bg}\times\eta\]
Combining the above two formulas, the calculation formula of the maximum safe annual emissivity $C_{atm}$ can be derived:
\[C_{atm}=\frac{M_{bg}\times\eta}{m_{eff}\times\tau}\]
Substitute specific values ​​for calculation:
Calculate the maximum allowable human load increment:
\[M_{limit}=6\times10^8\mathrm{~kg}\times0.05=3\times10^7\mathrm{~kg}\]
Substitute into the equilibrium equation to solve for the number of launches:
$$C_{atm} = \frac{3 \times 10^7 \, \text{kg}}{2000 \, \text{kg/launch} \times 3.75 \, \text{years}} = 4000 \, \text{launches/year}$$

\subsection{Modification of the Transportation Model}
Based on this model, we concluded that 4000 times/year is the theoretical physical upper limit of the earth's atmosphere without significant climate changes.
Therefore, the decision logic is revised to:
\[
S_{status} =
\begin{cases}
\text{Sustainable (Green)}, & \mathrm{if~}\max(L_t) \leq 4000, \\
\text{Environmental Hazard (Red)}, & \mathrm{if~}\max(L_t) > 4000.
\end{cases}
\]

\subsection{Conclusion}
Through the construction of the above formula, this model expands the original two-dimensional "time-cost" Pareto optimization into a three-dimensional "time-cost-environment" trade-off analysis.
Mathematical derivation shows that since the unit environmental cost $\mu $ of rocket transportation is constant and significantly greater than zero, while the unit environmental cost of the space elevator approaches zero, the mathematical solution to minimizing the environmental impact $E_{total}$ must point to a solution that maximizes the utilization rate of the space elevator.
This quantitatively proves that in a billion-ton Earth-moon transportation mission, extending the construction period to wait for the release of the space elevator's capacity is not only the optimal economic solution, but also the only path that the earth's ecosystem can withstand.
